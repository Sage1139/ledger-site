CHAPTER 1

Everyone chips in.
A pro invests.

A mutual fund pools money from thousands of investors. A professional fund manager uses that pool to buy a basket of stocks, bonds or both. In return you get units of the fund, which go up and down in value with the basket.

Even ₹500 gets you a slice of 50 or more companies, and instant diversification. Funds are run by asset management companies (AMCs) and regulated by SEBI.

CHAPTER 2

NAV: the price
of one unit.

NAV (Net Asset Value) is the value of everything the fund owns, minus its costs, divided by the number of units. Unlike a share, it updates once a day, after the market closes.

A low NAV doesn't mean a fund is "cheap". ₹10,000 buys more units at NAV ₹20 than at NAV ₹200, but if both funds rise 10%, you gain exactly the same.
CHAPTER 3

Types of funds
and their risk.

Every fund carries a SEBI riskometer label, from Low to Very High. Tap a fund type to see where it sits.

Equity

Mostly shares. Best for goals more than 5 years away. Large cap, mid cap, small cap and flexi cap.

Debt

Bonds and government securities. Steadier and lower returns. Good for 1–3 year goals.

Hybrid

A mix of shares and bonds. A middle path for moderate risk.

Index fund

Simply copies an index like the Nifty 50. No stock picking, very low costs.

Small cap

Small companies. Can grow fast, can fall 50%. Only for long horizons.

Liquid

Very short-term loans. A parking spot for emergency money.

CHAPTER 4

The hidden cost:
expense ratio.

Funds charge a yearly fee called the expense ratio, taken quietly out of the NAV. Every fund comes in two plans: Direct (you buy straight from the AMC, lower fee) and Regular (through a distributor who earns commission, higher fee). The gap looks tiny, but over decades it's huge.

CHAPTER 5

SIP: invest a little,
every month.

A SIP (Systematic Investment Plan) isn't a product. It's a way of investing in a mutual fund: a fixed amount is auto-debited from your bank every month and buys units at that day's NAV. No timing the market, no big lump sum needed.

The magic ingredient is compounding: your returns start earning returns of their own. In the early years it feels slow. Then it takes off.

CHAPTER 6

Why SIPs love
a falling market.

When the market falls, your fixed ₹5,000 buys more units. When it rises, it buys fewer. Over time your average cost per unit evens out. This is called rupee cost averaging, and it's why SIP investors shouldn't panic in a crash.
