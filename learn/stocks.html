CHAPTER 1

A share is a slice
of a real company.

Imagine a company is a giant pizza cut into crore of tiny slices. Each slice is a share. Own some slices and you own that part of the business: its factories, brands, cash and future profits.

Shareholders also get to vote on big decisions, like electing the board, and they receive a share of profits when the company pays a dividend.

CHAPTER 2

Why companies
sell shares.

A growing company needs money to build factories, hire people and expand. Instead of taking a loan, it can sell part of itself to the public. The first time it does this is called an IPO (Initial Public Offering). That's the primary market, and your money goes to the company.

After the IPO, the shares are listed on NSE and BSE. From then on, investors trade them with each other in the secondary market, which is what you see on your trading app every day. The company doesn't receive money from these trades.

IPOs can be exciting, but a big "listing gain" is never guaranteed. Many IPOs list below their issue price.
CHAPTER 3

How you actually
make money.

You earn from a stock in two ways. Capital gains: you sell for more than you paid. Dividends: the company shares part of its profit with you in cash, usually once or twice a year.

Gains are taxed. Shares sold within 12 months are taxed at 20% (short-term). Held longer than 12 months, gains above ₹1.25 lakh a year are taxed at 12.5% (long-term). These are the current rates, which the 2026 Budget left unchanged, but they can change.

CHAPTER 4

Market cap:
how big is the company?

Market capitalisation is share price × total number of shares. It's what the market thinks the whole company is worth. SEBI groups companies by size: the top 100 are large caps, the next 150 are mid caps, and the rest are small caps.

CHAPTER 5

P/E: is it cheap
or expensive?

A ₹3,000 share isn't automatically "costlier" than a ₹300 one. What matters is how much profit you're buying. EPS (earnings per share) is the company's yearly profit divided by its number of shares. The P/E ratio is price ÷ EPS: how many rupees you pay for every ₹1 of yearly profit.

Don't put all your eggs
in one basket.

Even great companies can crash: fraud, bad management, a new competitor. Diversification means spreading money across many companies and sectors, so one disaster doesn't wipe you out.
