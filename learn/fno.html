CHAPTER 1

What is a
derivative?

A derivative is a contract whose value comes from something else, called the underlying: a stock, or an index like the Nifty 50. You're not buying the thing itself. You're buying a deal about its future price.

Example from real life: a farmer agrees in June to sell rice to a mill in October at ₹30/kg. Whatever the market price is in October, the deal is fixed. The farmer is protected if prices crash, and the mill is protected if they spike. That's the original purpose of derivatives: hedging risk.

F&O contracts trade in fixed bundles called lots. One Nifty 50 contract is currently 65 units, so every 1-point move in Nifty is ₹65 per lot.

CHAPTER 2

Futures:
an obligation, with leverage.

A futures contract is an agreement to buy or sell at a fixed price on a future date. Buy a Nifty future and you profit if Nifty rises, and lose if it falls, rupee for rupee.

The catch is margin. You don't pay the full contract value. You put down a deposit, roughly 10–15%. That's leverage: a small move in Nifty becomes a big move in your money, in both directions. If losses eat your margin, your broker will demand more money or close your trade.

CHAPTER 3

Options: a right,
not an obligation.

An option gives the buyer the right, but not the duty, to buy or sell at a fixed price (the strike price) before expiry. You pay a fee for that right, called the premium.

Call option (CE)

The right to buy. You buy a call when you think the price will go up.

Put option (PE)

The right to sell. You buy a put when you think the price will go down.

An option buyer can lose at most the premium paid. The option seller (writer) collects the premium but can face very large losses. That's why sellers must put up big margins.

In the money (ITM)

The option already has real value. A call with strike below the market price, or a put with strike above it.

At the money (ATM)

Strike is about the same as the current price. These trade the most.

Out of the money (OTM)

No real value yet, only hope and time. Cheap, and most expire worthless.

CHAPTER 5

Time decay:
the silent killer.

An option's premium has two parts: intrinsic value (real value right now) and time value (the chance it becomes valuable before expiry). Every day that passes, time value melts away. This is called theta, and it speeds up near expiry.

That's a big reason option buyers lose. You can be right about the direction and still lose money if the move comes too slowly.

CHAPTER 6

Expiry day.

Every contract has an expiry date. Nifty 50 options currently expire every Tuesday (weekly), with a monthly contract expiring on the last Tuesday of the month. Index F&O is cash-settled: no shares change hands, only the profit or loss is paid.

On expiry, out-of-the-money options become worth zero. In-the-money options are worth exactly their intrinsic value. Expiry days are wild, with huge swings in premiums. Beginners should stay far away.

Every F&O trade also costs brokerage, exchange fees, GST, stamp duty and Securities Transaction Tax (STT). For small, frequent trades, these charges alone can turn a winning strategy into a losing one.
CHAPTER 7

Why most traders lose,
and how not to.

What goes wrong

Buying cheap OTM options hoping for a jackpot · trading on expiry day · no stop-loss · borrowing money to trade · revenge trading after a loss · following "tips" groups · ignoring charges.

What helps

Practise for months with virtual money · risk only 1–2% of capital per trade · always know your maximum loss before entering · keep a trading journal · treat F&O as a skill, not a lottery.
